                                             Abstract

Rapid urbanization in Nairobi continues to increase the demand for household services such as
plumbing, electrical repairs, and cleaning. However, existing service-matching platforms
primarily rely on distance and user ratings, often overlooking traffic congestion and varying road
conditions, resulting in unreliable provider arrivals and reduced user trust.

This study developed a context-aware recommendation system that predicts the arrival reliability
of household service providers in Nairobi from environmental and provider-related factors.
Because no real booking data were available, a synthetic dataset of 3,000 bookings, calibrated to
documented Nairobi traffic patterns and road quality, was used to represent the realities of urban
service delivery.

An XGBoost ensemble regression pipeline was trained on five inputs: distance, the congestion
multiplier of the provider’s corridor, time slot, the road quality of the provider’s area and the
provider’s completion rate. The data were split 80/20 and the hyperparameters were selected by
five-fold grid search on the training portion. On the 600 held-out records the model achieved an
RMSE of 0.1417, an MAE of 0.1160 and an R² of 0.4717, against RMSE values of 0.1872 for a
distance-only baseline and 0.1936 for a rating-only baseline. Congestion carried about half of the
model’s importance and distance less than a tenth.

The model was integrated into a working web prototype built with Next.js, FastAPI and
PostgreSQL with PostGIS. The prototype ranks available providers, explains each score and
supports booking, rescheduling and reviews, and automated backend tests passed. The results
hold for simulated data only: the system uses stored traffic patterns and not live traffic,
authentication is not verified on the server, and no study with users has been carried out.
Validation on real bookings is the main step needed before deployment.

Keywords: context-aware recommendation, ensemble learning, XGBoost, arrival reliability,
household service providers, Nairobi

1 Introduction

1.1 Background

The rapid growth of urban populations has increased the demand for on-demand home and
facility services, including electrical repairs, plumbing, and general technical maintenance. In
Nairobi, informal service economies provides most of these types of services. Customers look to
their social network (word of mouth) physical notice boards, or personal referral to find these
service providers; however, with the emergence of digital platforms for matching services with
digital providers both globally and locally, the hope is to bring formality to the service profession
and help users navigate through the discovery, comparison, and booking process for service
providers using web and mobile based interfaces.

Despite this digital shift, the quality and reliability of service delivery remains inconsistent.
Users continue to experience delays, no-shows, and substandard work even when engaging
providers with strong ratings and competitive pricing. This indicates that there is a structural gap
in how the current platforms are analysing and ranking service providers; they evaluate providers
in isolation without assessing the dynamic environmental and/or contextual conditions to which a
provider must operate within so as to successfully deliver on their promises when providing a
service. For example, service providers' ability to successfully complete a job and reach a client
can be affected by factors such as road conditions, flooding, traffic congestion, and localised
safety conditions.(Ricci et al., 2015).

Over time, recommendation systems in e-commerce and media streaming have evolved
significantly, transitioning from static, distance-based matching to intelligent, context-aware
recommendation models. These advanced theoretical models dynamically assess the situational
conditions under which a service is delivered. However, these improved theoretical methods
have not been effectively applied to the informal service sector, especially in urban regions of
developing countries where there are large variations in the quality of infrastructure. This project
bridges that gap by applying context-aware theories to a machine learning algorithm, providing
more reliable recommendations by assessing contextual risks drawn from stored time-slot and
corridor traffic patterns.

1.2 Problem Statement

Existing service-matching platforms recommend providers based primarily on proximity,
availability, and historical user ratings; a limited set of static parameters that do not account for
the real-world conditions that determine whether a provider can actually deliver as expected. In
Nairobi, where traffic congestion varies significantly by time of day, day of week, and road
corridor, straight-line distance is a poor predictor of actual travel time. A provider located three
kilometres away across a heavily congested route may take significantly longer to arrive than one
located six kilometres away on a clear road, yet current platforms treat both providers identically
from a proximity standpoint.

This mismatch between algorithmic rankings and real delivery performance leads to frequent
delays, missed appointments, and eroded user trust in service platforms. The core problem is the
absence of contextual awareness in provider-matching algorithms: none of the Kenyan platforms
reviewed in Chapter Two incorporates traffic conditions, time-based travel patterns, or road
quality into its recommendation logic, leaving users without a reliable basis for predicting
whether a chosen provider will arrive on time and complete the job successfully.

1.3 General Objective

The aim of this project is to design and develop a context-aware recommendation system for
household service providers in urban Nairobi that uses ensemble machine learning to integrate
traffic conditions, time-of-day travel patterns, road quality, and provider performance history
into a provider reliability score, enabling more accurate and dependable service provider
recommendations than proximity-based approaches.

1.3.1 Specific Objectives

     1.To identify the key factors that influence the reliability of household service providers in
       urban Nairobi, including traffic conditions, time of day, road quality, and past provider
       performance.

     2.To review existing Kenyan service-matching platforms and ML-based recommendation
       systems and identify gaps in contextual awareness and travel-time prediction.

     4.To evaluate the model's performance against distance-based and rating-based

1.4 Justification

There are three grounds that justify the development of this system: improved user experience,
economic efficiency, and technical contribution. From a user perspective, the persistent gap
between expected and actual service delivery represents a significant source of frustration,
particularly for households and small businesses that depend on timely technical services. A
system that more accurately predicts provider arrival reliability is expected to reduce the
incidence of missed appointments, wasted waiting time, and the cost of rescheduling. These
benefits are expected and were not measured in this study.

Economically, improved provider matching is expected to benefit all parties. Reliable providers
gain more consistent work opportunities; platforms reduce user churn caused by poor delivery
experiences; and the informal artisan economy can become more trustworthy and formalised.

From a technical standpoint, while Context-Aware Recommender Systems (CARS) are
theoretically well-established in e-commerce, their application to the urban household service
domain specifically integrating traffic-based travel time modeling and ensemble learning

                                                3
appeared largely unexplored in the Kenyan platforms and literature reviewed. This project
contributes a theoretically grounded approach, utilizing Gradient Boosting theory via XGBoost
to predict provider arrival reliability as a function of

contextual traffic features, time-of-day patterns, and historical provider performance.

1.5 Scope and Limitations

1.5.1 Scope of the Project

The system was delivered as a responsive web application that works on desktop and mobile
browsers and can be installed on a phone. It covers five categories of household service
providers in the Nairobi metropolitan area: electricians, plumbers, cleaners, technicians and
carpenters. The functionality includes registration and login for clients and providers, service
requests with a named area or an exact map location, context-aware provider ranking using the
trained XGBoost model with an explanation for each score, availability management for
providers, booking with status changes, cancellation, rescheduling, reviews, notifications and a
limited administrator console.

The machine learning component covers data preparation, feature engineering, model training
with XGBoost ensemble learning, and evaluation against distance-only and rating-only baseline
models. The trained model uses five inputs, one for each conceptual pillar: the straight-line
distance between client and provider, the congestion multiplier of the provider’s corridor for the
time slot, the time slot itself, the road quality of the provider’s area and the provider’s
completion rate. Other attributes are used around the model and are not model inputs: the
estimated travel time is calculated for display and for the explanation, the job date sets the day
type used to look up traffic patterns, and the provider’s rating is used by the rating-only baseline
and as an optional filter. A PostgreSQL database with the PostGIS extension manages provider
and spatial data. Provider onboarding and profile management are included within the system
scope.

1.5.2 Limitations in the Project

The system’s training data was synthetically generated from realistic Nairobi traffic patterns and
provider behaviour distributions. While the data is grounded in documented Nairobi road


                                                   4
conditions and congestion characteristics, it does not capture the full complexity of real-world
variability. Model performance may differ once the system is run against live data, and retraining
on real booking outcomes would be necessary over time to maintain predictive quality.

The system does not integrate real-time traffic APIs in its initial implementation; traffic features
are based on time-slot and corridor patterns derived from historical averages. Real-time API
integration is identified as a priority extension. The system does not cover every service provider
category, nor does it extend beyond the Nairobi metropolitan area. Payment processing and live
provider GPS tracking fall outside the scope of this project. Authentication is not verified on the
server, so the prototype does not provide production-grade access control.

1.5.3 Delimitations in the Project

The project deliberately excludes mobile application development (iOS/Android), focusing
solely on a responsive web-based interface to manage development scope and ensure cross-
device accessibility without platform-specific builds. Flood zone analysis, crime risk integration,
and real-time weather data while relevant to a broader risk-aware recommendation system are
beyond the scope of this project and are identified as future work extensions. The system does
not attempt to resolve disputes between users and providers, manage in-person service quality
inspection, or integrate with third-party payment gateways. These delimitations allow the project
to maintain a focused, technically rigorous implementation within the timeframe provided for the
project while delivering a genuinely novel and functional recommendation system.

2 Literature Review

2.1 Introduction

This chapter reviews existing literature and related systems relevant to the development of a
context-aware recommendation system for household service providers in urban Kenya. The
review is structured around four areas: the challenges that define the urban service delivery
problem in the Kenyan context, with particular focus on traffic congestion and travel time
variability; a critical analysis of three existing Kenyan service-matching platforms and how each
addresses or fails to address the contextual gap; the specific limitations and gaps that the
proposed system is designed to overcome; and a conceptual framework that maps the system’s
processing pipeline from raw inputs to ranked provider recommendations. The literature and
platform analysis presented in this chapter directly inform the design and architectural decisions
described in subsequent chapters.

2.2 Challenges Faced by Users of Service-Matching Platforms

The demand for on-demand household services in urban Kenya has grown substantially
alongside rapid urbanisation, with Nairobi’s population exceeding five million and continuing to
expand into densely populated peri-urban settlements such as Mathare, Kibera, and Ruiru (Kenya
National Bureau of Statistics, 2019). This growth has created a large and underserved market for
skilled tradespeople electricians, plumbers, cleaners, and general technicians many of whom
operate informally, without consistent access to clients or reliable income. The emergence of
digital service-matching platforms in Kenya over the past decade has sought to formalise this
market, but the platforms that have developed face a structural challenge: matching algorithms
designed for well-connected, infrastructure-stable environments do not translate effectively to
Nairobi’s complex urban geography.

A fundamental challenge in urban service delivery is the extreme variability of actual travel
times across the road network. The majority of service providers commute by public transit or
motorcycle, making their journey times highly sensitive to both road surface conditions and
prevailing traffic levels. Existing platforms fail to address this because they rely on the naive
theoretical approach of using straight-line (Haversine) distance as a proxy for travel time.



                                                 6
Theoretical studies in transportation modeling demonstrate that straight-line distance produces
significant errors in dense urban environments where road network topology, congestion, and
surface quality dominate actual journey duration. To overcome this structural challenge, travel
time must be modeled as a function of distance, free-flow speed, and a contextual congestion
multiplier that varies by route corridor and time of day.

Traffic congestion compounds this challenge further. Nairobi is consistently ranked among the
most congested cities in Africa, with average rush-hour commute times up to three times longer
than off-peak times along major corridors such as Mombasa Road, Thika Road, and Ngong Road
(TomTom International BV, 2025). A straight-line or basic GPS distance calculation the standard
used by most Kenyan service platforms is therefore a deeply unreliable proxy for actual travel
time and provider reachability. A provider located two kilometres away across a congested
junction may take longer to arrive than one located five kilometres away on a clear route, yet
current platforms rank these providers identically from a distance perspective.

Together, road quality variability and traffic congestion patterns define a category of contextual
challenge that is specific to the Nairobi urban environment and that existing recommendation
systems are structurally unable to address. Because platforms rely on static proximity as a
matching criterion, two providers at equal distances from a client are treated as equivalent
regardless of whether one faces a congested corridor and the other a clear bypass. A system that
can model these differences using time-of-day traffic patterns, road quality data, and historical
provider performance can produce materially more reliable recommendations than any distance-
based approach (Adomavicius et al., 2011).

2.3 Related Applications

Before analyzing specific local applications, it is necessary to establish the theoretical machine
learning frameworks that govern intelligent provider matching. Traditional recommender
systems generate suggestions based solely on user preferences and item attributes. However,
Context-Aware Recommender Systems (CARS) theory extends this paradigm by incorporating
situational variables such as time, location, and environmental conditions directly into the
recommendation function, consistently outperforming context-free models. Furthermore, to
process these complex, non-linear contextual variables, Ensemble Learning theory is utilized.
Techniques like Gradient Boosting

                                                 7
sequentially build decision trees to correct the residual errors of previous models. An optimized
implementation of this theory, XGBoost, is particularly effective for structured tabular datasets
and forms the theoretical baseline for modern, intelligent matching. Despite these theoretical
advancements, current Kenyan platforms exhibit significant gaps in their implementations.

2.3.1 Mama Fua App

Mama Fua is a Kenyan service-matching platform focused on cleaning, laundry, and fumigation
services, operating across Nairobi, Mombasa, and Kisumu. The platform is notable for its social
mission: it primarily onboards and empowers female workers, partnering with Cleaning School
Kenya to deliver standardised skills training before any worker is listed on the platform. Users
book services by selecting a service category and providing their location, after which the system
matches them with an available, trained provider in their area.

Mama Fua’s approach to service quality is human-centred rather than algorithmic: reliability is
guaranteed through vetting and training, not through intelligent matching. While this model
addresses the competency dimension of provider quality effectively, it creates a significant
contextual gap. The platform relies on standard location-based matching and does not
dynamically adjust estimated arrival times, provider rankings, or booking recommendations in
response to environmental conditions. During Nairobi’s heavy rainfall periods, when the ability
of a cleaner to commute via public transit is severely compromised, the platform offers no
mechanism to flag elevated delivery risk, recommend a closer alternative, or communicate
realistic adjusted arrival expectations to the user. The system’s quality guarantee is rendered
fragile by conditions the algorithm was never designed to consider.

2.3.2 Fundis App

Fundis App, also referred to as Fundilink, is a broad marketplace connecting users with skilled
tradespeople including plumbers, electricians, carpenters, and welders across Kenya. The
platform incorporates a two-way review system, allowing both clients and providers to rate each
other, and offers digital portfolios where providers can showcase completed work. Fundis also
provides a work quality guarantee for a defined period following job completion, introducing a
degree of accountability beyond the booking transaction.

Despite these features, the Fundis App’s core matching logic is based on straight-line
geolocation surfacing the nearest available provider to the user’s pin location. This approach,
while intuitive, is fundamentally flawed in the context of Nairobi’s urban geography. A plumber
located three kilometres away across a heavily congested corridor or through an area of poor
road infrastructure presents a materially higher operational risk than one located six kilometres
away on a clear, accessible route.



                                               9
The application lacks the spatial intelligence to compute route-based accessibility scores or to
factor road quality and real-time transit times into its ranking. The consequence is that a metric
the platform presents as a measure of convenience proximity is in practice an unreliable and
sometimes misleading predictor of actual provider reachability. This is the gap that route-aware
contextual features, such as corridor congestion and road quality, are designed to close.

2.3.3 Kazi Konekt

Kazi Konekt is an on-demand service application built around a dual-interface model comprising
a Worker App and a Client App, aimed at formalising Kenya’s gig economy. The platform
distinguishes itself from competitors through its use of an AI-first intelligent worker dispatch
system, which prioritises speed of response and worker availability. Providers are incentivised
through a badge and rating system that rewards completed jobs with higher visibility and
preferential dispatch, effectively building a dynamic ranking layer on top of availability-based
matching.

Kazi Konekt represents the most technically advanced of the Kenyan platforms reviewed, and its
AI dispatch layer is a meaningful step toward intelligent provider matching. However, the
algorithm is optimised for a single dimension: speed of dispatch based on availability and
proximity. It does not account for time-of-day traffic conditions or road quality on the route


                                                10
between a provider and a client. A concrete illustration of this limitation is the scenario where a
client in Embakasi requests a plumber at 8:00 AM on a weekday. Kazi Konekt’s system would
dispatch the nearest available provider without considering that Mombasa Road at that hour
typically operates at a fraction of its free-flow speed, meaning a provider six kilometres away on
a less congested route may realistically arrive sooner and more reliably than the nearest provider
across the gridlock. The absence of temporal traffic awareness in an otherwise intelligent system
means that the platform’s speed optimisation can, under predictable daily conditions, produce
suboptimal matching outcomes for both client and provider.

2.3.4 Context-Aware Recommender Systems

Adomavicius, Mobasher, Ricci, and Tuzhilin (2011) introduced the concept of Context-Aware
Recommender Systems (CARS), which extends traditional recommendation methods by
incorporating contextual variables such as time, location, and environmental conditions into the
recommendation process. The study demonstrated that considering contextual information
enables systems to produce recommendations that are more relevant to users' current situations,
overcoming the limitations of static recommendation approaches.

However, the work primarily provides a conceptual framework and discusses several application
domains rather than focusing on urban service-provider matching. Furthermore, the study does
not incorporate transportation variables such as traffic congestion or road quality, which are
critical factors


                                                11
in the Nairobi context. Nevertheless, the theoretical principles established in this work provide
the conceptual foundation for the present study.

2.3.5 XGBoost for Travel Time Prediction

Huang et al. (2020) investigated the use of tree-based ensemble methods, including XGBoost, for
travel time prediction using structured transportation datasets. Their study showed that ensemble
learning models effectively capture nonlinear relationships between temporal and spatial
variables and outperform simpler methods in prediction accuracy. The research highlighted the
suitability of gradient boosting techniques for handling complex interactions among traffic-
related features.

Despite its strong predictive performance, the study focused exclusively on estimating travel
duration rather than ranking alternative service providers according to their likelihood of
successful service delivery. The work therefore predicts trip characteristics rather than provider
reliability. Nonetheless, its feature engineering approach and the demonstrated effectiveness of
ensemble learning directly motivate the use of XGBoost in the present study.

2.4 Gaps in the Existing Applications

A structured comparison of the three platforms reviewed above reveals a consistent and
significant pattern of gaps that collectively define the problem this project addresses. The most
critical gap, present across all three platforms, is the complete absence of time-of-day traffic
awareness in provider matching. None of the reviewed platforms adjusts its recommendations
based on when a booking is made relative to known congestion patterns on the likely route
between provider and client. Proximity is used as a direct proxy for reachability, and a provider’s
aggregate rating is used as a proxy for reliability, when neither correlation holds consistently in
Nairobi’s traffic-variable environment. A provider ranked first at 10:00 AM on a Saturday may
be the worst choice at 7:30 AM on a Monday morning along the same corridor, yet current
platforms make no such distinction.

The second gap is the absence of contextual travel time modelling. Nairobi’s traffic conditions
change significantly by time of day, day of week, and road corridor. A booking made at 8:00 AM
on a weekday along Mombasa Road involves fundamentally different travel dynamics than one
made at 2:00 PM on a Saturday, yet none of the platforms reviewed model this difference. Kazi

                                                12
Konekt comes closest with its AI dispatch system, but even this operates entirely on internal
platform data, specifically worker availability and job history, without incorporating any traffic

signal or road quality variable into its matching logic.

Third, while Kazi Konekt employs an AI-based dispatch layer, no reviewed platform uses a
machine learning model to generate a multi-factor provider reliability score. The distinction
matters: dispatch optimisation selects the fastest-to-respond available provider, whereas
reliability scoring predicts which provider is most likely to successfully complete a job under
current conditions. The latter requires feature engineering that combines environmental, spatial,
and provider-profile data into a single predictive model a capability that XGBoost ensemble
learning is well-suited to provide (Chen & Guestrin, 2016).

Finally, none of the reviewed platforms exposes the basis of its matching decisions to users in a
transparent or explainable format. Users are presented with a list of providers without
understanding why a particular provider was ranked first, what risk factors were or were not
considered, or how conditions on the day of booking might affect the recommendation. This
opacity undermines informed decision-making and reduces the ability of users to calibrate their
expectations appropriately.

2.5 Conceptual Framework

The conceptual framework for this project describes how the system’s components interact to
transform raw inputs into a context-aware, ranked provider recommendation. It is designed to
directly address the four gaps identified in Section 2.4 by introducing time-of-day traffic
modelling, road quality scoring, ensemble machine learning, and an explainable output layer into
a unified processing pipeline.

Three categories of input initiate the pipeline. The first is the user request, capturing the service
type required, the client’s location, and the preferred booking time. The second is the provider
database, holding structured provider profiles including historical ratings, declared availability,
service specialisations, cost, and geographic base location stored in PostgreSQL. The third is
contextual traffic data: pre-computed traffic congestion multipliers by road corridor and time
slot, and road quality classifications by area, both stored in the PostgreSQL database and used to
estimate realistic travel times between each candidate provider and the client.

                                                 13
These inputs are processed through two analytical stages before reaching the machine learning

model. First, travel time estimation combines the provider’s distance from the client with the
congestion multiplier for the relevant corridor and time slot, producing a realistic estimated
travel time for each candidate provider. Simultaneously, feature engineering assembles the five-
feature vector for each candidate: distance, the congestion multiplier, the time slot, the road
quality of the provider’s area and the provider’s completion rate. The estimated travel time is
shown to the client and used in the explanation, but it is not a model input. The XGBoost
ensemble model receives these feature vectors and outputs a reliability prediction score for each
candidate, and the providers are ordered by that score alone. Availability removes candidates
before scoring, and the client may narrow the list by hourly rate, rating or verification status, but
these filters do not change the scores or the order.

The output is delivered to the user through the web-based interface, built in Next.js, which
presents the ranked provider list alongside the key contextual factors that influenced each
provider’s position. This transparency layer is the system’s response to the opacity gap identified
in the platform review: users can see not just who is recommended, but whywhether a provider
scored well because of a favourable time-of-day traffic profile, a clear route to the client, or a
strong historical completion record. Figure 2.4 shows the pipeline as originally conceived; in the
implemented prototype the model receives only the five features named above (Section 5.3.3).

3 Development Methodology

3.1 Introduction

This chapter outlines the methodology and strategies that were employed in the development of
the context-aware recommendation system for household service providers. It details the
quantitative experimental research paradigm governing the machine learning pipeline, alongside
the Agile software development methodology used to build the web-based Interactive Decision
Support System (IDSS). Furthermore, the chapter breaks down the system analysis and design
components, the specific development tools and techniques utilized, and the system deliverables.

3.2 Research Paradigm

This project adopts a quantitative experimental research design grounded in the CRISP-DM
framework. The experiment is structured around three model conditions that differ only in the set
of independent variables (features) provided to the recommendation algorithm. The dependent
variable across all conditions is the arrival_reliability_score a composite metric (0.0–1.0)
reflecting predicted provider on-time arrival and job completion probability.

3.2.1 Data Acquisition

The synthetic dataset was generated using a rule-based simulation framework implemented in
Python. The simulation parameters were derived from documented characteristics of the Nairobi
road network. Congestion multipliers and peak-hour time windows were calibrated against
TomTom Traffic Index data (TomTom, 2025), which records average rush-hour speeds of 16.7–
20.5 km/h and an evening congestion level of 98.8% across the city. Speed reduction factors on
major corridors were further validated against Uber Movement data, which confirms speeds on
Mombasa Road, Uhuru Highway, and Waiyaki Way run 60–80% below free-flow during peak
hours (Shah, 2021). Road quality classifications by neighborhood were grounded in the road
access deprivation framework applied to Nairobi by Haer et al. (2025) and surface type data from
the Kenya Roads Board's Road Inventory and Conditions Survey (KRB, 2023). The ten modeled
road corridors correspond to primary congestion routes identified in the JICA Nairobi Urban
Transport Master Plan Study (JICA, 2006) and confirmed by Daily Nation traffic analysis (Daily
Nation, 2025). This grounding ensures that while the dataset is


                                               16
synthetic, its distributional properties reflect the empirically documented realities of Nairobi.

3.2.2 Data Preprocessing

Before training, the dataset was checked for missing values, and none were found in the model
columns. The two categorical inputs, time_slot and provider_area_road_quality, are one-hot
encoded inside the saved pipeline, so the encoder is fitted on training data only. The numeric
inputs pass through unchanged, because gradient-boosted trees are unaffected by monotonic
scaling. The estimated travel time, which combines the Haversine (straight-line) distance with
the congestion multiplier of the corridor, is calculated for display and for the explanation. It is
not a model input, because the model receives the distance and the congestion multiplier
separately. The columns that are known only after a job ends were excluded to prevent leakage
of the outcome into the prediction.

3.2.3 Model Training

The experimental design manipulates the independent variables across controlled model
conditions. An initial candidate set of about twenty variables covered contextual features
(congestion multiplier, estimated travel time, road quality, time slot, day type), provider
performance features (rating, completion rate, experience, response time) and spatial distance. It
was reduced to five features, one per conceptual pillar, after testing showed that the reduced set
matched the performance of the larger one: distance, congestion multiplier, time slot, provider-
area road quality and provider completion rate. The dependent variable across all conditions is
the arrival_reliability_score, a continuous value from 0.0 to 1.0 that reflects the on-time arrival
and job completion of a provider in the synthetic data.

The models were trained under three conditions to isolate the effect of the contextual features.
Baseline A – Distance-Only: a linear regression predicts the score from the straight-line distance
alone, as a control that represents current platforms. Baseline B – Rating-Only: a linear
regression predicts the score from the provider’s historical rating alone, as a second control
without spatial or contextual data.

Proposed Model: an XGBoost ensemble model is trained on the five selected features. The
data were split 80/20 at random with a fixed seed, and the hyperparameters (learning rate,



                                                 17
maximum tree depth and minimum child weight) were selected by five-fold grid search on
the training portion only.

3.2.4 Model Validation & Testing

All three conditions are evaluated on the same held-out test dataset. The models are assessed
using two primary metrics:

       Root Mean Square Error (RMSE): Measures the average magnitude of prediction errors,
        penalizing large variances heavily. This is crucial for safety-relevant predictions like
        arrival

reliability.

       Mean Absolute Error (MAE): Measures the average absolute difference between
        prediction and actual value, indicating how far off predictions are on the 0–1 reliability
        scale.

Additionally, five-fold cross-validation was applied to the proposed XGBoost model. The
technique splits the dataset into five equal parts, training on four and testing on one, rotating until
every record has been tested. It indicates whether the model generalizes across different subsets
of the data and helps to detect overfitting. The core experimental hypothesis was that the
proposed model would produce lower RMSE and MAE scores than both baseline conditions,
which would support the inclusion of contextual traffic features.

This phase specified the functional and non-functional requirements of the system. The

                                               35

The client submits an area, a service type and a time, and the frontend forwards them to the

while bookings, ratings and profiles are read from PostgreSQL when a request arrives.

Figure 4.11 Logical Database Schema

The most important design decision is the corridor_areas table. The source data stores the areas
served by a corridor as one delimited string, which breaks first normal form and makes a query
such as “which corridors serve this area” impossible to index. The junction table holds one row
per traffic pattern and area pair, with a uniqueness constraint on the pair, and both columns are
foreign keys. Provider and client positions are stored as PostGIS point geometries (SRID 4326)
with GIST indexes so that spatial queries are efficient, while distances for the model are
computed with the Haversine formula. Integrity is enforced in the database itself: a check
constraint requires that a user has exactly the foreign key that matches the role, and the reliability
score in historical_bookings is constrained to the range 0 to 1.

5.3.1 Database and Data Implementation

The logical schema of Section 4.4.4 was implemented in PostgreSQL 16 with the PostGIS
extension. The schema file creates thirteen application tables, GIST indexes on the point
geometries of areas, clients and providers, and B-tree indexes on the foreign keys that the queries
join on. Later requirements were met by additive migrations instead of changes to the original
tables: exact locations (002), booking reviews (003), cancellation reasons (004), job dates (005)
reschedule proposals (006) and provider identity reviews (007). Each migration can be applied
again without harm.

The CSV files were loaded by the migration script. The pipe-delimited list of served areas in the
traffic file was split into rows of the corridor_areas junction table, so that 160 traffic patterns
produced 544 corridor and area pairs. Figure 5.17 shows the current PostgreSQL row counts
captured from the running database: 30 areas, 735 clients, 292 service providers, 160 traffic
patterns, 544 corridor-area mappings, 2,271 availability records and 6,000 historical bookings.

record training dataset described in Section 5.3.2. The screenshot alone does not establish why
the counts have increased or that the model was retrained on those additional rows. The tables

that   hold   application   activity   (users,   bookings,   notifications,   booking_reviews    and

proposal named three; technician and carpenter were added in the simulation and are offered by
the system, and Section 1.5.1 has been updated to match.

5.3.3 Data Preparation and Partitioning

The model uses five input features, one for each conceptual pillar of the design: distance_km for
spatial   separation,   congestion_multiplier    for   traffic,   time_slot   for   time   of   day,
provider_area_road_quality for road quality, and provider_completion_rate for provider history.
The set was reduced from a larger candidate set after testing indicated that the five features
matched the performance of the larger set. The estimated travel time is computed for display and
explanation but is not a model input, because it is a function of distance and speed that the model
already receives.

In line with DR-02, the columns that are known only after a job has ended (actual_travel_min,
delay_min, on_time, job_completed and client_rating_given) were excluded, since using them
would leak the outcome into the prediction. The data had no missing values in the model
columns, so no imputation was needed for training. Preprocessing is part of the saved pipeline:
the numeric features pass through unchanged and the two categorical features are one-hot
encoded, with unseen categories ignored. Because the encoder is inside the pipeline, it is fitted
on training data only, including inside each cross-validation fold.

                                                54
The records were split at random into 2,400 for training (80%) and 600 for testing (20%) with a
fixed seed of 42. The 600 test records were used only for the final comparison and not for tuning.
Hyperparameters were selected by five-fold cross-validation on the training portion. The split is
random across bookings and not grouped by provider, so the same providers appear in both parts.
The test therefore measures how well new bookings of known providers are predicted, and it
does not measure how well the model generalises to providers that it has never seen.

5.3.4 Model Training and Selection

Three conditions were compared, as set out in Section 3.2.3. Baseline A predicts the score from
distance alone and Baseline B from the provider’s rating alone, in both cases with a linear
regression fitted on the training records. The proposed model is an XGBoost regressor inside the
scikit-learn pipeline. A grid search with five-fold cross-validation over the learning rate (0.05
and 0.1), the maximum tree depth (3 and 5) and the minimum child weight (1 and 3) selected a
learning rate of 0.05, a depth of 3 and a minimum child weight of 3. The other parameters kept
their defaults and the random seed was 42. The untuned model, trained with default parameters,
obtained an RMSE of 0.1583 and an MAE of 0.1247 on the test set in the training notebook, so
tuning reduced the error further.

Table 5.11 compares the conditions on the same 600 test records. The proposed model has an
RMSE of 0.1417 and an MAE of 0.1160, which are 24.3% and 23.4% lower than those of
Baseline A and 26.8% lower than those of Baseline B on both measures. Its R² is 0.4717, against
0.0776 for distance alone and 0.0141 for rating alone. The target is continuous, so RMSE, MAE
and R² are the appropriate measures and accuracy is not used. These results satisfy requirement
NFR-01 on this dataset.

5.3.5 Recommendation Service

The recommendation service in api.py combines the database, the feature engineering module
and the saved model. When the service starts it loads the model and the reference tables into
memory. A POST /recommend request is first validated, so that a time slot outside the four
offered, a day type that contradicts the job date, or a date in the past or more than 60 days ahead
is rejected with status 422. Providers of the requested service type without an availability record
for the weekday and slot are removed, and a feature row is built for every remaining provider.
The model scores each row, the providers are sorted by score, a rule-based explanation is
attached, and the optional filters on rate, rating and verification are applied before the list is cut
to the requested length. A separate endpoint lists new providers so that a provider with no history
is not hidden (FR-08), and the booking endpoint recomputes the score on the server (FR-09).

5.4.4 Interpretation of the Test Results

The results show that the service behaves as specified for the cases that were tested, that invalid

that the model result can be reproduced. They do not show that the interface is easy to use, that

and the suite was written by the author who wrote the code, so it is likely to share the author’s
assumptions.

5.7.2 Discussion and Error Analysis

The model beat both baselines by a wide margin because, in the simulated data, reliability
depends mainly on congestion, provider history and their interaction with the time of day, and a
straight line through distance or rating cannot represent that. The comparison should be read with
care for two reasons. The baselines are linear regressions on one variable, so the gap combines
the effect of the richer feature set with the effect of a non-linear model, and a linear model on the
five features, or an XGBoost model on distance alone, was not run to separate the two. The
untuned XGBoost model (RMSE 0.1583) lies between the baselines and the tuned model, so
tuning accounted for a further 10.5% reduction in RMSE.

Table 5.19 shows where the errors are on the 600 test records. They are largest where the system
is needed most: in the evening rush, under heavy congestion and on poor roads. The clearest
pattern is that the model is least accurate for the least reliable bookings. For the 60 records whose
actual score is below 0.5, the RMSE is 0.2589 and the model over-predicts by 0.226 on average,
while it under-predicts the most reliable bookings by 0.086. This is the compression towards the
mean that was visible in Figure 5.19, and it means that a risky booking can be ranked higher than
it deserves.

Limitation             Effect on the findings

Synthetic data         All 3,000 training records were simulated. The results describe the
                       behaviour of the system on simulated Nairobi data and say nothing about
                       real providers, and the feature importance reflects relationships that were
                       built into the simulator.

Evaluation design      The split is random across bookings and not grouped by provider, so the
                       test measures new bookings of known providers and not new providers.
                       Only one split was held out, and the baselines are single-variable linear
                       models.

Stored traffic         Congestion comes from stored corridor and time-slot averages. The
patterns               system has no live traffic feed and cannot react to an incident on a given
                       day.

Target and outcome     The model predicts a synthetic reliability score. It does not predict
                       arrival time, and no study measured missed appointments or client
                       satisfaction.

No user study          Usability, accessibility and the clarity of the explanations were not tested
                       with users.

Security               Authentication is not verified on the server, so the prototype must not be
                       used with real accounts. Administrator verification reviews profile and
                       contact details and does not check a government identity document. No
                       deletion or retention policy exists (DR-08).

Environment            Performance was measured in one environment with sequential requests
                       and no load test, and the hosted deployment was not independently
                       verified.
